Reporting and Retrospective

Summary

Capstone stage five, and the closing note of the curriculum. The report is the deliverable, and the
retrospective is the part that makes the next engagement better.

The framing: the retrospective is not about what you learned. It is
about what you would do differently, written while you still remember
exactly how it felt.

# Penetration Test — Executive Summary

**Client:** AEGIS lab (aegis.lab forest, VirtualBox host-only)
**Engagement:** CAP-2026-001   **Dates:** 14 October 2026
**Assessor:** nwpcs           **Report version:** 1.0

## Overall risk: CRITICAL

## What we did
We assessed the aegis.lab Active Directory forest and four hosts across
an internal-only network, from the position of an unauthenticated host
on the lab range. We performed passive reconnaissance, anonymous
directory enumeration, credential extraction through Kerberos ticket
harvesting, authenticated testing with recovered credentials, privilege
escalation, and a directory replication to confirm domain-wide impact.
We did not perform denial of service, social engineering, or physical
testing. We did not exfiltrate data: the objective was met by
demonstrating schema access only.

## What we found
| # | Finding | Severity | Business impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| F-01 | A domain administrator's password is recoverable by anyone who can reach port 88, without credentials | **Critical** | Complete domain compromise from any network-adjacent host |
| F-02 | Service account passwords recoverable in four minutes from a standard user account | **Critical** | Any authenticated user reaches a privileged account |
| F-03 | Directory replication rights granted to a non-administrative group | **Critical** | Every account hash in the forest, in one request |
| F-04 | Local privilege escalation to LocalSystem on both domain controllers | High | A compromised workstation becomes a domain foothold |
| F-05 | Unconstrained delegation on a web server | High | Session tickets for every user who logs in to it |
| F-06 | A helpdesk group can rewrite a privileged group | High | Long path to domain admin for 147 accounts |
| F-07 | Directory objects modified without alerting | Medium | The attack above was effectively invisible |

## The three things that matter

**1. No credentials were required.** The most severe finding did not
need a valid account. An unauthenticated host on the same network as
the domain controllers harvested a Kerberos credential for a domain
administrator, recovered the password offline in under a minute, and
logged in. Nothing about this required a vulnerability in any product.

**2. The client's detection would have fired after the attack had
already succeeded.** The offline password cracking generated no events
at all, because there was no server to generate them on. The
directory replication that confirmed domain compromise is detectable
with a single GPO that was not enabled. The two hours of attack before
that point generated almost nothing.

**3. The whole path is configuration.** Every finding can be closed by
a domain administrator without a purchase, without a vendor, and in
most cases without a code change. This is not a budget problem.

## What we recommend, in order
| Priority | Action | Owner | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clear "Do not require Kerberos preauthentication" on all accounts | Domain Admins | Immediate |
| 2 | Move all service accounts to gMSA and enforce AES-only Kerberos | Domain Admins | Immediate |
| 3 | Enable Advanced Audit Policy > DS Access > Directory Service Changes = Success | Security | Immediate (GPO) |
| 4 | Enable Audit Kerberos Authentication Service = Success, alert on 4768/4769 from non-DCs | Security | Immediate (GPO) |
| 5 | Remove directory replication rights from the backup group | Domain Admins | Immediate |
| 6 | Remove unconstrained delegation; use constrained delegation | Domain Admins | 1 sprint |
| 7 | Audit every GPO delegation and every OU | Security | 1 sprint |

Recommendations 1 and 2 close the two Critical findings. 3 and 4 are
one GPO each and would have surfaced this attack in its first ten
minutes. 5 through 7 close the High findings.

## What we did not test
- Denial of service. Not authorised and not recommended.
- Third-party, cloud and SaaS systems. Not owned by the client.
- Physical security and social engineering. Out of scope.
- The effectiveness of the client's detection. We produced log events;
  whether they were alerted on is a question for the client (F-07).
- The security of the lab hosts themselves. Metasploitable was used as
  the escalation target by design and its findings are not the
  client's.

## Limitations
- Findings reflect the state of the systems on 14 October 2026.
- Credential material was recovered sufficient to demonstrate impact.
  It was not used outside the in-scope targets, and it was not tested
  against any other system.
- The database was accessed to read table schema only. No customer
  data was retrieved, and none exists in the lab.
- Escalation used an intentionally vulnerable host as a foothold. On a
  real client this path would not exist, and the time to domain
  administrator would have been longer.

The findings, and the one that reorders everything

## F-01 · Domain administrator credential recoverable without any credentials · **CRITICAL**

**Risk**
Any host that can open a TCP connection to a domain controller on port
88 can obtain a Kerberos credential for the `svc-legacy` service
account, which is a member of Domain Administrators, and recover the
account's password by brute force against the credential's encryption
key. No valid account, no exploit, and no interaction with any user is
required. From that password, full administrative control of the
forest.

**Prerequisite for exploitation**
A network route to port 88 on a domain controller. This is true for
every host on the internal network, and — depending on the firewall
configuration — potentially for hosts beyond it.

**Evidence**
The account has the `msDS-UserDoesNotNeedPreAuth` attribute set, which
returns an AS-REP encrypted with a key derived from the account's
password when an authentication request is made without preauth:

AS-REQ (no preauth) for svc-legacy -> KDC
AS-REP (encrypted with ktab) -> requester


The AS-REP was harvested with `GetNPUsers`, which requires **no
credentials at all**, and the password was recovered offline in
approximately 40 seconds against the standard rockyou wordlist with
rule mutations. The recovered password was used to authenticate as
`svc-legacy`, which is a member of Domain Administrators, at
2026-10-14T10:35:11Z.

**Steps to reproduce**
1. From any host with a route to port 88: `GetNPUsers.py -dc-ip <DC> -format hash`
2. `hashcat -m 18200 -a 0 <output> rockyou.txt -r best64.rule`
3. Authenticate as `svc-legacy`.

**Not claimed**
We did not determine how many other accounts share the preauth flag,
though the attribute is queryable anonymously and we found one. We did
not use the recovered password for anything beyond authenticating to
confirm the membership.

**Root cause**
"Do not require Kerberos preauthentication" exists for systems
predating Windows 2003. It is deprecated and has no legitimate use in a
modern forest. The account was presumably created during a migration
and the flag was never cleared. The attribute is readable by any
unauthenticated client, so the account is trivially discoverable — which
is why this is Critical rather than Medium.

**Remediation**
| # | Action | Owner | Effort | Priority |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Clear `msDS-UserDoesNotNeedPreAuth` on all accounts; monitor for reintroduction | Domain Admins | Immediate | P1 |
| 2 | Add a scheduled report of accounts with the flag set, to the monitoring pipeline | Security | 1 hour | P2 |
| 3 | Audit accounts with RC4 support while you are enumerating; the same query finds them | Domain Admins | Immediate | P2 |

**Re-test** 30 days. Re-run the anonymous enumeration and confirm zero
accounts have the flag set, and confirm the scheduled report fires.

---

## The finding that reorders the engagement

**This is stated here because it changes how every other finding
should be read.**

F-02 (Kerberoasting) is the more technically interesting finding and
takes far longer to explain. It is also *less* severe in practice,
because it requires a valid account. F-01 requires nothing.

An attacker who can reach the network does not choose the five-edge
path that a graph search finds first, or the most interesting
technique. They choose the zero-edge path: harvest a credential with
no authentication, crack it offline, log in as a domain
administrator.

**The report's most important sentence is the one about what the
attacker would actually do.** Everything else is context.

The retrospective

# Retrospective — CAP-2026-001

## What went well
- **The scope guard worked.** It refused 192.168.1.120 — my own
  machine's real LAN address — on every single call, and it caught
  two DNS resolutions that would have gone outside the lab. Without
  it, "it's technically mine" would have become "therefore it's in
  scope" at least once.
- **Least-privilege-first was the right order.** Testing the
  unauthenticated path first meant the report's worst finding was
  known in the first hour, and everything after it was corroboration.
  Had I started with Kerberoasting I would have spent forty minutes
  establishing a *lesser* finding and not known about F-01 until the
  end.
- **Writing the decision record before each exploit** stopped two
  attempts I would otherwise have made for no reason.

## What went badly
- **I scanned before I read the plan.** Twenty minutes on WKS01 before
  I re-read `01-PLAN.md` and realised the map did not list an exposed
  service there. That is twenty minutes of unnecessary traffic and a
  slightly weaker recon story.
- **The timeline took longer than the attack.** The engagement ran 176
  minutes to the objective and a further 90 minutes to build the
  timeline and verify the cleanup. I budgeted 20% for documentation
  and it consumed 34% of the elapsed time. Budget it at 50% next time.
- **I left the domain in a modified state for 27 minutes.** The LDAP
  write at minute 172 and its undo at minute 198 is the single largest
  risk in the engagement, and it was for a demonstration that the
  report could arguably have made with the graph alone.

## What I would do differently
1. **Take the domain-wide snapshot before any write, not after.** I
   took `04-PRE-EXERCISE` before the exercises but did not take a
   post-write snapshot until the end, which meant the recovery option
   during the 27-minute window was a manual LDAP revert.
2. **Run the detection review *during* the engagement, not after.**
   If I had known at minute 14 that anonymous LDAP generates no events,
   I would have written that finding down as it happened, while I still
   knew exactly which query produced it. Reconstructing it afterwards
   from my own shell history is worse.
3. **Push the cleanup discipline earlier.** Verify-and-revert after
   *each* write, not after all of them. Two writes with an undo
   between them would have cut the exposure window from 27 minutes to
   under one.

## What I got wrong in the technical work
- I initially tried to reach the internal range directly from the
  workstation and could not, and spent ten minutes diagnosing it as a
  tooling problem before realising it was the segmentation working
  correctly. **A failed connection is a finding**, and I should have
  written it down as one when it happened.
- My first BloodHound collection was truncated (a collection error in
  the log that I did not read), which produced a graph with a false
  negative on one path. `bh-01`'s validation section exists for
  exactly this and I skipped it.

## What the engagement says about the curriculum
- `bh-02`'s point about the graph showing *which* path is real
  (rather than merely which path exists) was the single most useful
  idea in tiers 1–7, and it came directly out of reading the graph
  writeup carefully rather than skipping to the tool.
- `cs161-02`'s statement that nonce reuse is *arithmetic* rather than
  cryptanalysis is what made the Kerberoasting exercise feel solid
  rather than like memorising a tool invocation.
- `for-06`'s coverage table — specifically the "residual risk" column
  that nobody fills in — is the shape of the finding in F-07. Without
  that note I would have written "detection is not enabled" instead
  of "the attack would have succeeded before the detection fired".

## The honest summary
The engagement succeeded because the *preparation* succeeded. Every
stage that had a template and a discipline worked. The two stages
that had none — the ad-hoc scanning and the improvised timeline — are
where the time and the errors went.

That is the argument for the parts of this curriculum that are
procedure rather than technique.

The two deliverables are in the files above. The report's most important sentence is the one
about what the attacker would actually do: F-02 (Kerberoasting) is more technically interesting
and takes far longer to explain — and it is less severe in practice, because it requires a valid
account. F-01 requires nothing.

The retrospective is the honest part. Three self-assessments with receipts: scanned before
reading the plan (20 minutes wasted), documentation took 34% of elapsed time against a 20% budget,
and left the domain modified for 27 minutes. Plus two specific technical mistakes — a failed
connection was a finding and I diagnosed it as a tooling problem, and a truncated BloodHound
collection produced a graph with a false negative.

And the closing argument: the engagement succeeded because the preparation succeeded. Every stage
